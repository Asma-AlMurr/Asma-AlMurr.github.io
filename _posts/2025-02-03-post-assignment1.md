## Introduction: A Journey into Horror Through Digital Humanities  

I’ve never really been fond of horror. In fact, the genre always felt like uncharted territory, a strange world of shadowy figures and bloodcurdling thrills that never quite resonated with me. While my friends rave about movies or novels that deliver the perfect scare, I’d often pass on the experience. Yet this assignment provided the perfect excuse to dive headfirst into horror and uncover what makes it so captivating for so many people.  

During my analysis, I decided to explore two horror classics, Bram Stoker’s *Dracula* and Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein*, along with an AI-generated horror text I created through Chat GPT titled *Shadows in the Machine*. These texts, taken from Project Gutenberg, are considered classics for a reason—they grapple with timeless themes such as the dangers of ambition and the tension between the known and the unknown. This analysis aims to explore the enduring appeal of horror classics by analyzing the thematic and stylistic elements that have allowed *Dracula* and *Frankenstein* to withstand the test of time, shaping the genre for generations and revealing what truly makes a story "classic."

## Understanding Horror Beyond Surface-Level Fears  

Before this project, my understanding of horror was limited to a few tropes: jump scares, eerie settings, and the occasional ghost story. I associated the genre with pure adrenaline and visceral reactions rather than a deep exploration of the word choice in classic horror novels.  

In *Dracula* and *Frankenstein*, I always admired their narrative craftsmanship from afar but never conducted distant readings of the texts. This assignment challenged me to look beyond the surface-level storyline and focus on the highest frequency words through the lens of digital humanities, using tools like Voyant Tools and RMarkdown notebooks. I was able to delve into patterns and stylistic elements that I would have missed in a straightforward read. For instance, the computational analysis revealed recurring thematic elements such as the persistent use of time-related language (words like “night,” “day,” and “time”) and the frequency of character names, which act as anchors for the narratives. These details hinted at the careful construction behind what might initially seem like simple horror stories.  

## The Gothic Foundations of Horror: A Comparative Analysis  

To better understand horror’s evolution, I compiled a **corpus of three texts**: *Dracula*, *Frankenstein*, and *Shadows in the Machine*, an AI-generated story. The first two, drawn from Project Gutenberg, represent the **bedrock of Gothic horror**.  

- *Dracula* is steeped in atmosphere and dread, with its eerie descriptions of the night and its portrayal of an otherworldly creature that feasts on human blood.  
- *Frankenstein*, on the other hand, delves into the **ethical and emotional consequences of scientific ambition**, questioning the limits of human endeavor.  
- The wildcard, *Shadows in the Machine*, created through a prompt I fed to ChatGPT, represents a **modern reimagining of horror**—a genre that has evolved alongside technological advancements.  

It incorporates contemporary digital motifs, such as references to “code,” “static,” and “digital” phenomena, which contrast sharply with the more traditional language and settings of the Gothic classics. This juxtaposition allowed me to explore how the conventions of horror are being reinterpreted in the digital age and whether the same themes of terror and suspense persist when the source is a machine-generated narrative.

## Uncovering Patterns in Horror Through Computational Analysis  

To objectively analyze these texts, I employed **Voyant Tools and RMarkdown notebooks**, which allowed me to **perform distant reading** and detect patterns that would be difficult to see through traditional close reading. The tool provided visualizations such as **word clouds, frequency charts, and graphs**, revealing interesting insights about each text.  

For example, the overall corpus of three documents contained **246,384 total words** with **13,163 unique word forms**. Among the texts:  

- *Dracula* emerged as the longest with **165,629 words**  
- *Shadows in the Machine* was the shortest, with only **2,353 words**  

A closer look at vocabulary density revealed that:  

- *Shadows in the Machine* had the highest density (**0.391**)  
- *Dracula* had the lowest vocabulary density (**0.060**)  

Additionally, readability scores varied:  

- *Shadows in the Machine* scored **10.308**, making it relatively easy to read  
- *Dracula* had a much lower score of **5.458**, indicating greater linguistic complexity  

Without digital tools, such detailed **quantitative insights** would have been difficult to obtain.  

## Thematic Patterns: Time as a Horror Device  

A key trend that emerged from my computational analysis was **the repeated emphasis on time-related language** across both classic and AI-generated horror. Words like **“night,” “day,” and “time”** were among the most frequent terms, with **“night” appearing over 120 times in *Dracula*** alone.  

These words are not merely decorative; they function as **narrative devices that build mood, enhance tension, and mark pivotal moments** in the story. Additionally, character-specific words—such as:  

- **“Van Helsing,” “Lucy,” and “Mina”** in *Dracula*  
- **“Elizabeth,” “Clerval,” and “Justine”** in *Frankenstein*  

acted as **anchors**, reinforcing each novel’s distinct world and themes. The AI-generated *Shadows in the Machine* differed in vocabulary, frequently using modern horror terms like **“code,” “static,” and “digital”**, but still retained a **similar focus on tension and the unknown**. This suggests that **while horror’s language has evolved, its thematic foundations remain the same.**

## Bridging the Past and the Present in Horror  

One of the most fascinating aspects of this project was how it **bridged the gap between past and present horror storytelling**. The **Gothic classics** were written in an era when **scientific discovery and supernatural folklore coexisted**, reflecting anxieties about the **unknown, the consequences of overreaching ambition, and the tension between tradition and modernity**. These themes remain relevant today, albeit in new forms.  

The AI-generated *Shadows in the Machine* is a **modern iteration of Gothic horror**, replacing **supernatural monsters with digital ones**. This demonstrates that horror is not just about scares—it is about **confronting our deepest insecurities and reckoning with the unpredictable consequences of our actions**.  

## Conclusion: A New Appreciation for Horror  

This assignment has been a **transformative experience**. Initially hesitant about horror, I explored *Dracula*, *Frankenstein*, and *Shadows in the Machine* using **digital humanities tools like Voyant Tools and RMarkdown**, uncovering patterns that **traditional reading alone could not reveal**.  

Horror is more than just **jump scares**—it is a **deliberate combination of word choice, structure, and deep-rooted human fears**. My findings suggest that **while horror evolves, its core themes remain the same**.  

This project **demonstrated the power of digital humanities**, allowing me to see patterns that are often **invisible in traditional literary analysis**. Now, I recognize that horror is not just about fear—it is about **exploring human limitations and aspirations**.
