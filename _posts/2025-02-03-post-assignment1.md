### Asma Al Murr  
Professor David Wrisley

Intro to Digital Humanities  

## Introduction: 

I’ve never really been fond of horror. In fact, the genre always felt like uncharted territory, a strange world of shadowy figures and bloodcurdling thrills that never quite resonated with me. While my friends rave about movies or novels that deliver the perfect scare, I’d often pass on the experience. Yet this assignment provided the perfect excuse to dive headfirst into horror and uncover what makes it so captivating for so many people. During my analysis, I decided to explore two horror classics, Bram Stoker’s “Dracula” and Mary Shelley’s “Frankenstein”, along with an AI-generated horror text I created through Chat GPT titled “Shadows in the Machine”. These texts taken from Project Gutenberg are considered classics for a reason, they grapple with timeless themes such as the dangers of ambition and the tension between the known and the unknown. This analysis aims to explore the enduring appeal of horror classics by analyzing the thematic and stylistic elements that have allowed “Dracula” and “Frankenstein” to withstand the test of time, shaping the genre for generations and revealing what truly makes a story "classic."

Before this project, my understanding of horror was limited to a few tropes: jump scares, eerie settings, and the occasional ghost story. I associated the genre with pure adrenaline and visceral reactions, rather than a deep exploration of the word choice in classic horror novels. In “Dracula” and “Frankenstein” I always admired their narrative craftsmanship from afar but never did distant readings of the texts. This assignment challenged me to look beyond the surface-level storyline and focus on the highest frequency words through the lens of digital humanities, using tools like Voyant Tools and RMarkdown notebooks. I was able to delve into patterns and stylistic elements that I would have missed in a straightforward read. For instance, the computational analysis revealed recurring thematic elements such as the persistent use of time-related language (words like “night,” “day,” and “time”) and the frequency of character names which act as anchors for the narratives. These details hinted at the careful construction behind what might initially seem like simple horror stories.

## Corpus:
To better understand horror’s evolution, I compiled a corpus of three texts: Dracula, Frankenstein, and Shadows in the Machine, an AI-generated story. The first two, drawn from Project Gutenberg, represent the bedrock of Gothic horror. “Dracula” is steeped in atmosphere and dread, with its eerie descriptions of the night and its portrayal of an otherworldly creature that feasts on human blood. “Frankenstein”, on the other hand, delves into the ethical and emotional consequences of scientific ambition, questioning the limits of human endeavor. Both texts are rich with themes of isolation, and the clash between modernity and ancient superstition. The wildcard, “Shadows in the Machine”, created through a prompt I fed to ChatGPT, represents a modern reimagining of horror, a genre that has evolved alongside technological advancements. It incorporates contemporary digital motifs, such as references to “code,” “static,” and “digital” phenomena, which contrast sharply with the more traditional language and settings of the Gothic classics. This juxtaposition allowed me to explore how the conventions of horror are being reinterpreted in the digital age, and whether the same themes of terror and suspense persist when the source is a machine-generated narrative.

## Analysis:
To objectively analyze these texts, I employed Voyant Tools and RMarkdown notebooks, which allowed me to perform distant reading and detect patterns that would be difficult to see through traditional close reading. The tool provided visualizations such as word clouds, frequency charts, and graphs that revealed interesting insights about each text. For example, the overall corpus of three documents contained 246,384 total words with 13,163 unique word forms. Among the texts, “Dracula” emerged as the longest with 165,629 words, while “Shadows in the Machine” was the shortest, with only 2,353 words. A closer look at vocabulary density revealed that “Shadows in the Machine” had the highest density (0.391), suggesting that despite its brevity, it was packed with distinctive language. In contrast, “Dracula” had the lowest vocabulary density (0.060), which is typical for a longer, more elaborate narrative. Furthermore, the average words per sentence varied, with “Frankenstein” averaging 23.1 words per sentence and “Dracula” averaging slightly less at 19.4 words per sentence. The readability index also varied, with “Shadows in the Machine” scoring 10.308 and “Dracula” at a notably lower 5.458, indicating differences in complexity and accessibility between the texts. It's important to note that I wouldn't have been able to do a distant reading with an in depth analysis manually, and using this tool was incredibly helpful for that.  

Furthermore, after utilizing the RMarkdown notebook, a key trend that emerged from my computational analysis was the repeated emphasis on time-related language across both classic and AI-generated horror.  In both “Dracula” and “Frankenstein”, words like “night,” “day,” and “time” were among the most frequent terms, “night” alone appeared over 120 times in “Dracula”. These words are not merely decorative; they function as narrative devices that help build mood, enhance tension, and demarcate critical turning points in the stories. Additionally, the distinctive words identified in each text such as “van,” “helsing,” “lucy,” and “mina” in “Dracula” and “elizabeth,” “clerval,” “justine,” and “cottage” in “Frankenstein”, provide further evidence of how each narrative constructs its unique world and reinforces its thematic concerns. By using these digital tools, I was able to quantify and visualize these recurring motifs. The ability to see which words dominated the texts, and how their frequencies shifted across different narratives, offered a new layer of understanding. For instance, the top 10 highest frequency words provided by the RMarkdown notebook analysis revealed that while some words like “said” and “time” were common across the corpus, the specific names and thematic words differed significantly between the classics and the AI-generated text. These quantitative insights allowed me to appreciate the meticulous craft behind the classics and to understand the unique, sometimes jarring, stylistic choices of the AI-generated text.

One of the central questions I grappled with was: What factors make horror so addicting? and how can I use my new digital toolbox to draw conclusions to this question?  As Barry Smith notes in his article on LLM’s and practical knowledge: 
>“Today, every university – and I mean every university, is putting together its plans for a new AI building. Researchers everywhere, and in whatever field, are assembling plans for using AI (or at least for bringing about the appearance of using AI) in their work.” (Smith, 7). 

Classic horror narratives are often celebrated not only for their ability to scare but also for their exploration of deep, existential themes. Both novels tackle the dangers of unchecked ambition. In “Frankenstein”, the creation of life without ethical boundaries serves as a cautionary tale about the perils of playing God. Similarly, “Dracula” explores the invasion of the unknown into the familiar, creating a palpable sense of dread as characters confront forces beyond their control. For instance, the word clouds showed large clusters around terms associated with darkness and time, which are central to the mood of Gothic horror. In contrast, the AI-generated text “Shadows in the Machine” offered a modern twist on these themes. While it maintained the essential elements of horror, such as the presence of an ominous digital entity and the fear of losing control, it did so through a vocabulary that is distinctly modern. Words like “digital,” “code,” and “static” evoke a sense of technological dread that is entirely different from the fog-shrouded landscapes of “Dracula” or the scientific ambitions of “Frankenstein”. Yet, the underlying tension remains the same: a confrontation with the unknown, whether it takes the form of a supernatural vampire or a rogue AI.

## Process:
The process of analyzing these texts using digital humanities tools challenged my preconceived notions. Initially, I was intimidated by the idea of using the RMarkdown notebooks as it was completely foreign to me. Running the packages, and learning to navigate the obstacles of the never ending (yes or no) pop up reminded me of what learning a new language felt like. However, as I progressed, I began to see how these tools could uncover layers of meaning that are hidden beneath the surface of the text. Gathering and preparing the corpus was another challenge. I had to ensure that all three texts were properly formatted and tokenized so that the software could accurately analyze them. This process taught me a lot about data wrangling and the importance of careful preparation in digital analysis. Once the texts were ready, I dove into the analysis, and the insights that emerged were both unexpected and enlightening.

Working alone provided a unique kind of freedom. I could experiment with different approaches without the constraints of group dynamics or conflicting ideas. This independence allowed me to explore the data fully and develop a deep understanding of the texts. The results from Voyant Tools and the RMarkdown notebook not only validated my initial hypotheses about recurring themes and motifs but also revealed subtleties that I would have likely missed during a traditional close reading. For example, one of the most surprising findings was the prominence of mundane words like “said” and “time” over more overtly horrifying terms. I had expected words like “vampire” or “monster” to dominate the texts, but the data showed that atmosphere and setting captured through time-related language, played a more significant role in creating the sense of dread that defines Gothic horror. This observation aligns with theories advanced by scholars such as Geoffrey Rockwell and Stefan Sinclair, who argue in their article that computer-assisted text analysis shifts our focus from individual details to larger patterns of meaning. This is evident when they state: 
>“The analysis of datasets are beyond our capacity to read individually” (Rockwell and Sinclair, 114)

 This illustrates how through computational tools, we are able to engage with these texts in a way that transcends traditional literary analysis. 

One of the most fascinating aspects of this project was how it bridged the gap between the past and the present. The Gothic classics were written in an era when scientific discovery and supernatural folklore were in constant dialogue. Their enduring popularity lies in their ability to capture the anxieties of their time, fears of the unknown, the consequences of overreaching ambition, and the struggle between tradition and modernity. Yet these themes remain relevant today, albeit in new forms. The AI-generated text is a case in point. While it employs modern terminology and digital imagery, it echoes the core concerns of its Gothic predecessors. The text grapples with the idea of a malevolent digital presence, a modern monster born not of superstition or unbridled science but of our own technological creations. It poses questions about the limits of artificial intelligence and the ethical boundaries of innovation, much like “Frankenstein” questions the boundaries of human ambition and the repercussions of playing God. Similarly, the work of Nitin Liladhar Rane and Saurabh Choudhary highlights how AI can serve as both a collaborator and a critic in the creative process. As stated in the article:
>“In the realm of Humanities, ChatGPT acts as a crucial research assistant, supporting scholars in tasks like data analysis, literature review, and information synthesis” (Rane and Choudhary, 1).

 My own use of ChatGPT to generate a horror story exemplifies this, demonstrating how AI not only participates in storytelling but also reinterprets horror through a contemporary lens. Ultimately, this reinforces the idea that horror is not merely about the superficial thrill of a scare; it is about confronting our deepest insecurities and reckoning with the unpredictable consequences of our actions, whether in the age of Gothic literature or the era of artificial intelligence.

## Conclusion:
In exploring what makes these texts enduring, I found that their appeal lies in a delicate balance of familiarity and innovation. “Dracula” and “Frankenstein” are steeped in the conventions of their time, yet they continuously reinvent those conventions to speak to new generations. For instance, the data revealed that in “Dracula”, the word “night” was a recurring motif, used over 120 times. This is not accidental, it reflects the novel’s reliance on darkness as a metaphor for the unknown and the dangerous. These stylistic choices, when viewed through the lens of computational analysis, reveal a deliberate effort to manipulate mood and tension, ensuring that every element of the text contributes to the overarching atmosphere of horror. By comparing these classics with the AI-generated “Shadows in the Machine”, I could see that while the language and imagery have shifted, the fundamental elements of horror remain the same. The modern text’s emphasis on digital motifs such as “code” and “static” is a contemporary adaptation of the timeless fear of the unknown. Even as the vocabulary evolves, the core impulse behind the genre like the exploration of boundaries, both literal and metaphorical, persists.

Overall, this assignment has been a unique experience to work on since at first I didn't really understand its structure, but now that the pieces have fallen into place I can see how the insights I gained from class were translated into this analysis. Initially hesitant about horror, I explored “Dracula”, “Frankenstein”, and “Shadows in the Machine” using digital humanities tools like Voyant Tools and RMarkdown. I discovered that horror is more than just pointless jumpscares, it’s a blend of  distinctive word choice, and stylistic approaches that reflect deep human anxieties. Through computational analysis, I identified recurring patterns, such as the heavy use of time-related language like "night" and "day" in “Dracula” and “Frankenstein”, which heightened suspense. The AI-generated “Shadows in the Machine” introduced modern digital motifs like "code" and "static," yet retained similar themes of the unknown and loss of control, proving that horror evolves while maintaining its core fears. This project reshaped my understanding of horror’s allure and it forces us to confront what we cannot control, whether supernatural, scientific, or digital. Beyond horror, this assignment demonstrated the power of digital humanities, allowing me to see patterns invisible in traditional reading. My technical skills in computational analysis improved, and my appreciation for horror deepened. Now, I recognize that horror is not just about fear but about exploring human limitations and aspirations.
