### Reflections on Digital Literacy: A University Student’s Perspective

As an 18-year-old university student at a liberal arts college, I constantly navigate the intersection of technology and critical thinking. Specifically in taking this course I learned that digital literacy extends beyond basic computer or social media proficiency; it involves comprehending and effectively utilizing digital tools in both academic and personal contexts. Digital Literacy as defined by [www.edweek.org](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/what-is-digital-literacy/2016/11) is 
> "the ability to use information and communication technologies to find, evaluate, create, and communicate information." (Loewus, 2017)

This signifies that while technology plays a central role in my daily routine, I realize that in order to grasp the full depth of digital literacy, especially in a world increasingly shaped by artificial intelligence, data analysis, and computational thinking, I need to analyze what tools are available me and how I can use them to my best advantage. In the context of digital humanities, this definition takes on a larger meaning. I’ve come to understand that true digital literacy involves analyzing how to shift a piece of information and transform it, whether turning historical texts into interactive maps, translating statistical data into visual narratives, or recontextualizing archival documents digitlally. This process not only reveals the power of form in shaping content but also challenges me to think critically about visulization, communication and interpretation in the digital age.

In each context, it involves more than technical proficiency, it is about enging with various tools to be able to create and transform inform information in a manner that is purposeful and tailored to specific audiences or mediums. In other words it helps us look at content in one form through another lens or medium to enrich our digital experince. As an Interactive Media major, I use platforms like Adobe Creative Suite, Photoshop, and Adobe Animate to design interactive content while others in my major use Figma because it allows real-time collaboration, kind of like Google Docs, but for designing websites, apps, and other digital interfaces. My classmates in Film and New Media work with software like Adobe Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve for video editing. This illusrtates how in one way or another we all rely on digital platforms to create our work, communicate with our audience, and collaborate with peers.

In additon to this, Digital literacy is relvent in our day-to-day life since it shapes modern social interactions, influencing how we build relationships, express ourselves, and engage with communities in digital spaces. Platforms such as Discord, Slack, and Trello facilitate collaboration on group projects and extracurricular activities. Recognizing how these platforms function, along with their advantages and limitations, is a crucial skill, enabling us not only to engage in digital spaces but also to analyze and shape them.

### Exploring Digital Humanities and New Ways of Learning

Digital humanities bridge traditional academic disciplines with computational methods, opening new possibilities for research and analysis. Before taking courses in this field, I viewed textual analysis as mainly a close-reading exercise. However, exposure to machine learning tools for sentiment analysis and topic modeling has changed my perspective. Now, I see literature not just as individual texts but as part of larger, analyzable systems. One reading source in particular stood out to me in class, it was David Berry and Anders Fagerjord's [On the Way to computational Thinking](https://www.wiley.com/en-nl/Digital+Humanities%3A+Knowledge+and+Critique+in+a+Digital+Age-p-9780745697659). Computational thiking was one of those terms you feel like you could break down in your head, but actually defining it was much harder than anticpated due to the many layers beneth the terms. As stated by Berry and Fagerjord,

>"The ability to reflect on computation and computational practices as a whole… linked to a set of practices, or what we might call literacies, related to technical, and particularly algorithmic or computational, know-how.” (Berry & Fagerjord, 2017).

This signifies how computational thinking isn’t just about knowing how to code or use digital tools—it’s about understanding how these tools work and how they influence the way we think, learn, and communicate. Berry and Fagerjord describe it as more than a technical ability; it’s a kind of practical understanding that helps us critically reflect on how technology shapes knowledge and culture. It also involves the process of finding a clear, logical way to solve complex problems. This starts by breaking the problem into smaller, more manageable parts, noticing patterns, removing unnecessary details, and then creating a repeatable, step-by-step method to solve it.

This is similar to how in collaborative projects, my peers and I bring different perspectives to the table. A Computer Science student might propose an algorithm to sort data, while I might focus on the ethical implications of data collection. These discussions help us develop well-rounded views on digital tools, and how we can apply them to computational thinking.

### Assessing My Digital Skills and Areas for Growth

Even though I am becoming more comfortable with digital research tools, I recognize that there is still much to learn. While I have a basic understanding of text mining and data visualization, I want to gain deeper knowledge in natural language processing and data storytelling. With AI increasingly influencing creative fields, I am curious about how it can be used responsibly in writing and storytelling.

I also want to explore the ethical aspects of digital work. How do biases in AI shape the way information is processed and shared? How can we ensure that digital tools do not reinforce existing inequalities? Digital literacy is not just about computational skills but also about developing a critical mindset that allows us to evaluate technology's impact on society.

However one limitation I must brush on that I learned early on in class is that a myth about the digital environment which expresses that it is permanent once something is uploaded online, it exists forever in a weightless, invisible space. However, this perception is misleading. As the [Digital Humanities overview](https://guides.nyu.edu/digital-humanities) we read in the begining of the semester explains, 

>“Common myths about the digital environment are that it is stable, even archival (e.g. permanent) and that it is ‘immaterial’ (e.g. not instantiated in analog reality). Every actual engagement with digital technology demonstrates the opposite. Digital formats are ephemeral, vulnerable, and depend upon elaborate material infrastructures” (Blanchette, 2011).

This highlights the reality that digital content is far from indestructible; it is constantly at risk of disappearing due to outdated file formats, broken servers, or lost data. Additionally, the idea that the digital is immaterial overlooks the physical systems—like data centers, fiber-optic cables, and energy-consuming devices—that keep the digital world running. Understanding the material and fragile nature of digital technology is essential for anyone working in digital humanities, where preserving and managing digital information requires just as much care and planning as traditional archival work.
