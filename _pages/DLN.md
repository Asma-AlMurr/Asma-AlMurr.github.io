### Reflections on Digital Literacy: A University Student’s Perspective

As an 18-year-old university student at a liberal arts college, I constantly navigate the intersection of technology and critical thinking. Specifically in taking this course I learned a lot about digital literacy, and how it extends beyond basic computer or social media proficiency; it involves comprehending and effectively utilizing digital tools in both academic and personal contexts to enrich our undertsanding of the digital world we live in. Digital Literacy as defined by [www.edweek.org](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/what-is-digital-literacy/2016/11) is 
> "the ability to use information and communication technologies to find, evaluate, create, and communicate information." (Loewus, 2017)

This definition signifies that in order to grasp the full depth of digital literacy, especially in a world increasingly shaped by artificial intelligence, data analysis, and computational thinking, I need to analyze what tools are available me and how I can use them to my best advantage. In the context of digital humanities, this definition takes on a larger meaning. I’ve come to understand that true digital literacy involves analyzing how to shift a piece of information and transform it, whether turning historical texts into interactive maps, translating statistical data into visual narratives, or recontextualizing archival documents digitlally. This process not only reveals the power of form in shaping content but also challenges me to think critically about visulization, communication and interpretation in the digital age.

### How Digital Literacy Shapes Content Creation and Social Interaction

Digital literacy plays a vital role across a wide range of fields, from the humanities to business and healthcare. In each context, it involves more than technical proficiency, it is about enging with various tools to be able to create and transform inform information in a manner that is purposeful and tailored to specific audiences or mediums. In other words it helps us look at content in one form through another lens or medium to enrich our digital experince. As an Interactive Media major, I use platforms like Adobe Creative Suite, Photoshop, and Adobe Animate to design interactive content while others in my major use Figma because it allows real-time collaboration, kind of like Google Docs, but for designing websites, apps, and other digital interfaces. My classmates in Film and New Media work with software like Adobe Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve for video editing. This illusrtates how in one way or another we all rely on digital platforms to create our work, communicate with our audience, and collaborate with peers.

In additon to this, Digital literacy is relvent in our day-to-day life since it shapes modern social interactions, influencing how we build relationships, express ourselves, and engage with communities in digital spaces. Platforms such as Discord, Slack, and Trello facilitate collaboration on group projects and extracurricular activities. While I connect with communities through Instagram and TikTok, my friends in Art History curate virtual exhibitions using Google Arts & Culture. Recognizing how these platforms function, along with their advantages and limitations, is a crucial skill, enabling us not only to engage in digital spaces but also to analyze and shape them.

### Exploring Digital Humanities and New Ways of Learning

Digital humanities bridge traditional academic disciplines with computational methods, opening new possibilities for research and analysis. Before taking a course in this field, I viewed textual analysis as mainly a close-reading exercise. However, exposure to machine learning tools for analysis and topic modeling has changed my perspective. Now, I see literature not just as individual texts but as part of larger, analyzable systems. One reading source in particular stood out to me in class, it was David Berry and Anders Fagerjord's [On the Way to computational Thinking](https://www.wiley.com/en-nl/Digital+Humanities%3A+Knowledge+and+Critique+in+a+Digital+Age-p-9780745697659). Computational thiking was one of those terms you feel like you could break down in your head, but actually defining it was much harder than anticpated due to the many layers beneth the terms. As stated by Berry and Fagerjord computational thinking is,

>"The ability to reflect on computation and computational practices as a whole… linked to a set of practices, or what we might call literacies, related to technical, and particularly algorithmic or computational, know-how.” (Berry & Fagerjord, 2017).

This signifies how computational thinking isn’t just about knowing how to code or use digital tools—it’s about understanding how these tools work and how they influence the way we think, learn, and communicate. Berry and Fagerjord describe it as more than a technical ability; it’s a kind of practical understanding that helps us critically reflect on how technology shapes knowledge and culture. It also involves the process of finding a clear, logical way to solve complex problems. This starts by breaking the problem into smaller, more manageable parts, noticing patterns, removing unnecessary details, and then creating a repeatable, step-by-step method to solve it.

This is similar to how in collaborative projects, my peers and I bring different perspectives to the table. A Computer Science student might propose an algorithm to sort data, while I might focus on the ethical implications of data collection. These discussions help us develop well-rounded views on digital tools, and how we can apply them to computational thinking.

### Assessing My Digital Skills and Areas for Growth

Even though I am becoming more comfortable with digital research tools, I recognize that there is still much to learn. While I have a basic understanding of text mining and data visualization, I want to gain deeper knowledge in natural language processing and data storytelling. With AI increasingly influencing creative fields, I am curious about how it can be used responsibly in writing and storytelling.

I also want to explore the ethical aspects of digital work. How do biases in AI shape the way information is processed and shared? How can we ensure that digital tools do not reinforce existing inequalities? Digital literacy is not just about computational skills but also about developing a critical mindset that allows us to evaluate technology's impact on society.

However one limitation I must brush on that I learned early on in class is that a myth about the digital environment which expresses that it is permanent once something is uploaded online, it exists forever in a weightless, invisible space. However, this perception is misleading. As the [Digital Humanities Course Book]([https://guides.nyu.edu/digital-humanities](https://www.academia.edu/120744656/The_Digital_Humanities_Coursebook) we read in the begining of the semester explains, 

>“Common myths about the digital environment are that it is stable, even archival (e.g. permanent) and that it is ‘immaterial’ (e.g. not instantiated in analog reality). Every actual engagement with digital technology demonstrates the opposite. Digital formats are ephemeral, vulnerable, and depend upon elaborate material infrastructures” (Drucker, 2021).

This highlights the reality that digital content is far from indestructible; it is constantly at risk of disappearing due to outdated file formats, broken servers, or lost data. Additionally, the idea that the digital is immaterial overlooks the physical systems—like data centers, fiber-optic cables, and energy-consuming devices—that keep the digital world running. Understanding the material and fragile nature of digital technology is essential for anyone working in digital humanities, where preserving and managing digital information requires just as much care and planning as traditional archival work.

### Reflection on Digital Platforms, AI, and My Experience

Recently, I’ve immersed myself in a diverse set of new digital platforms Zotero, Kepler, and Orange Data Mining, each playing a distinct role in how I think, create, and collaborate. Zotero, for instance, has completely transformed how I manage my research references. I honestly wish I had discovered it sooner; during past semesters, I spent hours manually organizing citations, often losing track of articles or re-finding the same sources repeatedly. Zotero now acts like a personal librarian, one that’s present across all my devices and grows with me. Orange Data Mining opened up a whole new world for me: I started feeding it images from my photography archives and was amazed to discover how clustering techniques could visually reveal recurring themes, certain color palettes, framing choices, or emotions that I hadn’t consciously planned. Orange has its limits, of course. With more complex datasets, its user interface can feel clunky and doesn’t flow. But its ability to give immediate visual feedback makes it a valuable sketchpad for creative pattern discovery.  I also realized I starting using things like Google Workspace more, it has become my go-to collaborative backbone, it’s where my ideas evolve from scattered thoughts into structured presentations, essays, and design plans, especially during big projects.

One of my biggest regrets is not learning Kepler earlier. During my "Sacred Cities" class last semester, I had to map key religious landmarks in Jerusalem and Mecca. I did what I could using basic tools, but I now realize how Kepler’s layering and unique mapping features could have brought my project to life in a much more dynamic way. It's one of those moments where hindsight really stings, where I can see how a tool might have changed not just the quality of the work, but also how I felt making it. As mentioned in the article [How AI is hijacking art history] (https://theconversation.com/how-ai-is-hijacking-art-history-170691)

> “For years, university humanities departments have been gradually squeezed of funding, with more money funneled into the sciences.” (Drimmer,2021)

This made me question why we can’t use technology and science in tandem with the teachings of digital humanities, instead of treating them as separate or opposing forces. Why must funding be a zero-sum game? If anything, the intersection of these fields offers some of the most promising intellectual terrain of our generation. We live in a moment where AI can translate ancient texts, where mapping software can visualize centuries of religious migration, and where machine learning can detect stylistic shifts across thousands of poems. Yet, somehow, these tools are rarely placed in the hands of students studying culture, identity, or art. This disconnect feels not only wasteful, but short-sighted. Digital humanities isn’t about rejecting technology; it’s about using it to ask deeper, more human questions. It’s about using algorithms to uncover bias in archives, or harnessing data visualization to make hidden histories visible. It allows artists, writers, and thinkers to reimagine storytelling, not just through content, but through form. By limiting investment in the humanities, we risk narrowing the scope of how technology is applied in the world. We risk reinforcing systems that prioritize efficiency over meaning, output over understanding. The goal shouldn’t be to modernize the humanities to make them more scientific; it should be to humanize science by infusing it with the imaginative rigor that humanities scholars bring to the table. 


In digital environments, I’ve become increasingly confident navigating software, databases, even when I have never used the tool before, for example coding in R for data manipulation and statistical visualization. Picking up coding has felt like learning a new language, not just for machines, but for expressing my own ideas in a more precise, structured way. More than anything, I’ve learned that data is never just neutral; it always comes embedded in a narrative, shaped by context, by ethics, and by the stories we choose to tell with it. Still, I’m far from mastering the art of integration in terms of all the new tools I’ve gained in this course. I often struggle to make my tools collaborate with one another. However, I understand this is part of the process, and I’m learning to embrace the rhythm of trial and error.

Perhaps what excites me the most is learning and creating alongside AI. Working with GPT feels like having a sharp, fast-thinking co-pilot, one that can brainstorm, summarize, and even surprise me with phrasing I hadn’t considered. However, it also needs major human guidance. AI still hallucinates facts, repeats phrasing, or generates surface-level content that sounds polished but says very little. It reminds me of what the article [False Positives] (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7580233) argues about AI and data:

> “Text analysis on a very large scale is clearly happening, and analytical tools are being developed by governments and corporations; they are spending millions if not billions of dollars on developing infrastructure and tools for analyzing and storing metadata and text” (Rockwell & Sinclair, 2016)

GPT, in that sense, reflects our larger relationship with data, we now live in a world where it’s no longer just a tool, but it’s being sewn into the fabric of our society. It’s important to note however that knowing when to zoom in and when to zoom out is crucial. Yes, GPT is absolutely getting better at tone, flow, and structure, but without human judgment, it risks flattening complex thought into generic dull words. For me, the future lies not in replacing our creativity with AI, but using it as an assistant  to expand the boundaries of what we thought was capable.
